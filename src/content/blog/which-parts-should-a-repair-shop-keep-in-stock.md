**A repair shop should keep in stock the parts it replaces most often on the vehicles it sees most, in quantities that cover a typical week or month of work. That usually means service items such as filters, wiper blades, and bulbs, plus common wear and failure parts for its top vehicles. Order slower, vehicle-specific parts as jobs come in.**

The goal of a parts shelf is simple: finish more jobs the same day without tying up too much cash in boxes. This guide walks through how to choose what goes on the shelf, how much to hold, and how to keep the shelf working as your customer mix changes.

## Why keep parts on the shelf at all?

Every job that waits for a part costs something: a bay that sits idle, a customer who has to come back, or a vehicle that stays overnight. Keeping the right parts on hand lets you:

- finish routine services without waiting for a delivery
- add small, related items to a job while the vehicle is already in the bay
- pay a lower unit price by buying in quantity instead of one at a time

The risk is the opposite problem: parts that sit for months, or that turn out not to fit the vehicles you actually see. The answer is to stock from your own data, not from a generic list.

## How do you decide which parts to stock?

Start with what you have already done. Your work orders are the best guide to what you will do next.

1. **Export your recent jobs.** Pull the last three to six months of work orders.
2. **Count parts by part number.** Group by the replacement part, not just the category, because one filter or coil may fit several models.
3. **Rank by how often each part was used.** The top of the list is your shelf.
4. **Check the vehicles behind the numbers.** Note which makes and models produced the most work.
5. **Separate fast movers from one-offs.** Anything used once in six months is usually better ordered as needed.

## Which part types move fastest?

Every shop is different, but some categories tend to come up again and again because they are replaced on a schedule or wear out regularly. The table below groups common shelf candidates by why they move.

| Category | Why it moves | Stocking note |
|---|---|---|
| Cabin and engine air filters | Replaced on a maintenance schedule | Small and light; easy to hold by the case |
| Oil filters, drain plugs, and washers | Used at every oil change | Hold enough for your daily oil-change volume |
| Wiper blades | Seasonal and frequent | Stock the lengths your top vehicles use |
| Bulbs | Frequent, quick add-on | Multi-packs cover several vehicles |
| Trim clips and fasteners | Break during many jobs | The small parts that stop a job when you run out |
| Ignition coils and spark plugs | Common on higher-mileage engines | Stock for your most common engines |
| Brake pads | Regular wear item | Front and rear sets are different parts |
| Sway bar links and tie rod ends | Frequent on high-mileage vehicles | Usually replaced in pairs |
| Shop supplies | Used every day | Gloves, fuses, and terminals |

Use the table as a starting point, then let your own counts decide.

## How much of each part should you hold?

A simple rule is to hold enough of each fast mover to cover your normal demand until the next delivery arrives, plus a small buffer.

1. Take the number you used over your review period and divide by the number of weeks.
2. Multiply by the number of weeks between orders.
3. Add a buffer for busy weeks.
4. Round to a sensible quantity, such as a full case, when the volume price makes it worthwhile.

Review the numbers every quarter. Seasonal parts, such as wiper blades before winter, may need a temporary increase.

## How do you keep the shelf organized?

A well-organized shelf saves time on every job.

- **Label bins by part number,** not just by description, so the right part is easy to find.
- **Keep the fitment list with the bin.** A note of the vehicles each part fits avoids mix-ups.
- **Pull new stock to the back.** Use older stock first.
- **Record what you take.** A simple sheet or your shop software tells you when to reorder.

## What should you not stock?

Leave these off the shelf and order them per job:

- parts that fit only one vehicle you rarely see
- large or heavy parts with low turnover
- anything you cannot confirm fits the vehicles in your area

When in doubt, start small. It is easier to add a part to the shelf after it proves itself than to clear out boxes that never move.

## The short version

Stock from your own work orders, focus on the parts you replace every week, and hold enough to cover the gap between deliveries. Use the volume tiers for true fast movers, buy slower parts as needed, and review the list every quarter.
