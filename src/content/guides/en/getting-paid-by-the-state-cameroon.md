For many Cameroonian SMEs, the State, local authorities and public companies are major clients. They are also the clients who pay most slowly. This guide describes the circuit a public invoice follows, what you control at each stage, and how to organise your cash around delays you don’t control.

## How long does the State take to pay?

According to the Treasury Directorate of the Ministry of Finance, reported by Financial Afrik in February 2026, the Cameroonian State took **more than 200 days** in 2025 to pay its suppliers’ invoices, compared with 160 days in 2024 and 120 days in 2023. The ministry attributes these delays to a mismatch between spending commitments and available cash, and notes that payments are concentrated at the end of the financial year.

According to the business press, at the end of March 2026 the State owed more than 520 billion FCFA to its suppliers of goods and services.

These figures have a direct consequence for SMEs, even those that don’t work with the State: when the largest client in the country pays this late, the whole chain waits. Your private clients pay you late because they are being paid late themselves.

## Which stages does a public invoice go through?

The financial regime of the State, set by Law no. 2018/012 of 11 July 2018, governs how public spending is executed. An expense goes through four phases, handled by different people. The French terms are the ones you will hear:

| Stage | Who handles it | What happens | What you do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Commitment** (*engagement*) | The authorising officer | The administration commits the expense: contract or purchase order. | Keep the signed order and its reference number. |
| **2. Verification** (*liquidation*) | The department receiving the work | The work is checked and the amount confirmed. | Provide a signed acceptance report and an invoice that matches the order. |
| **3. Payment order** (*ordonnancement*) | The authorising officer | The order to pay is issued. | Note the date and the person in charge, follow up in writing. |
| **4. Payment** (*paiement*) | The public accountant | The Treasury pays. | Check your bank details on file and reconcile the payment the day it arrives. |

Remember the split between the authorising officer, who decides the expense, and the public accountant, who pays. An invoice “approved” by the department that received the work is not yet a paid invoice: it still has to receive a payment order, then be paid.

## What can you control?

You won’t change the State’s cash position. You can stop your file from adding weeks to a delay that is already long. An incomplete file goes back to the bottom of the pile.

**The complete file, first time:**

- the contract or purchase order, with its reference;
- the signed delivery note or acceptance report;
- an invoice matching the order line by line;
- every document the contract requires, up to date;
- bank details identical across all documents;
- a dated copy of every submission, with a receipt stamp.

## How do you track a public invoice?

In your unpaid-invoices tracker, add a “stage” column for each public invoice: commitment, verification, payment order or payment. At each contact, write down the date, the person’s name and what was said. Confirm in writing the same day.

This tracking has two effects. It lets you chase the right person at the right stage of the circuit. And it gives you, at any time, a realistic view of when the money will arrive, which you need to manage your cash. Your aging report should keep public clients separate: their age does not mean the same thing as a private client’s.

## Which mistakes send a file back to the bottom of the pile?

The same mistakes come up again and again, and each can cost several weeks:

- **A different amount** on the invoice and the purchase order, even by a few francs.
- **Bank details that change** from one document to another, or an outdated bank certificate.
- **An expired administrative document** at the time of submission, when the contract requires up-to-date documents.
- **An invoice sent to the wrong department**, or submitted without a receipt stamp: you then cannot prove the submission date.
- **No signed acceptance report**: without a record that the work was done, verification cannot take place.

A five-minute checklist before each submission avoids this back-and-forth.

## How do you follow up with an administration?

A written, courteous and precise reminder works better than a phone call that leaves no trace. Ask where the invoice is in the circuit, and who to contact for the next stage. The template below is in French, the language of most administrations in Cameroon.

```modele
Objet : Facture n° [N] du [date], marché / bon de commande n° [référence]

Madame, Monsieur,

Nous avons déposé le [date] la facture n° [N] d’un montant de [montant] FCFA, relative au [marché / bon de commande] n° [référence], avec le PV de réception signé le [date].

Pourriez-vous nous indiquer à quelle étape du circuit se trouve ce dossier, et si une pièce complémentaire est attendue de notre part ?

Nous restons à votre disposition et vous remercions de votre attention.

[Nom, fonction, téléphone]
```

In English: it gives the invoice number, date, amount and contract reference, states that the signed acceptance report was submitted, and asks which stage the file has reached and whether any document is missing. Keep a dated copy of every reminder, with a receipt stamp when delivered by hand.

## What should you do as the year end approaches?

The Ministry of Finance notes that payments are concentrated at the end of the financial year. For an SME, the practical consequence is simple: a file that is complete and already verified by then stands a better chance of being included in year-end payments than one still stuck at the first stage. Review your public invoices several weeks before the end of the year, and complete whatever is missing.

## How do you protect your cash?

- **Put a figure on the wait before signing.** A contract paid at 200 days is not worth the same as one paid at 60 days. The calculator shows what the wait costs if you finance it with an overdraft.
- **Balance your portfolio.** If public clients make up a large share of your revenue, make sure private clients pay on time: solid contracts, reminders on fixed dates.
- **Don’t let public delays spread to your suppliers.** Warn them early and negotiate payment schedules rather than paying late without notice.

## Should you turn down public contracts?

Not necessarily. A public contract brings volume and a reference. The question is whether your cash can carry the wait without putting salaries and suppliers at risk. If it can’t, there are levers before signing: negotiate an advance at the start when the contract allows it, split the work into invoiceable instalments, or limit the share of public clients in your revenue.

## Worked example

*Fictional example.* A construction company carries out a 30,000,000 FCFA public contract. If the invoice is paid at 200 days instead of 60, that is 140 extra days of waiting. Financed by an overdraft at 14% a year, they cost about 30,000,000 × 14% × 140 ÷ 365 ≈ **1,611,000 FCFA**. If an incomplete file adds another 30 days, the cost rises by about **345,000 FCFA**.

The figure matters less than the method: estimate the cost of the wait before committing, and don’t make it longer yourself.
